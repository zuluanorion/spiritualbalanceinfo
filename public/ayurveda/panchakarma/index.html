Panchakarma

The deep purification of Ayurveda

AyurvedaThe three doshasPanchakarmaRasayana

WHY WE PURIFY

It all begins with the fire

If you have come this far feeling tired, heavy or foggy in the mind, I want to begin with the simplest thing: your fire. To understand Panchakarma, we have to begin there.

In Ayurveda, your health depends not only on what you eat, but on what your body is able to transform. That force is called agni, the digestive fire: it turns food into your own body, into blood, muscle, bone and energy. And it does not digest food alone: it also digests what you see, what you feel and what you live through.

The classical texts teach that life, strength, the color of the skin, enthusiasm, ojas (the subtle essence that sustains your natural defenses) and prana (the life force) all depend on this fire. When agni goes out, life goes out; when it is balanced, one lives a long and healthy life; when it is disturbed, illness appears. That is why tradition calls it the root of life.

Your fire changes according to which of your three vital energies, the doshas vata, pitta and kapha, is moving it. Of the four states of the fire, only the first digests well; in the others, the fire falls short or burns too hot. See which one you recognize yourself in:

AMA

What is not digested stays

When the fire is not strong enough, what we eat is never fully transformed. A heavy, sticky, cloudy residue remains, which Ayurveda calls ama, a word that means “raw” or “uncooked.”

It is born from eating without hunger or before the previous meal has been digested, from cold, heavy or badly combined foods, from irregular mealtimes. But not only from food: tradition teaches that even the purest food is poorly digested when it is eaten with worry, sadness, fear or anger. And what we could not digest from life also leaves its residue: a loss, an anger we swallowed, an experience that was too much.

The signs of ama

  • A whitish coating on the tongue on waking
  • Heaviness after eating
  • Waking up tired, even after sleeping
  • A foggy, slow mind
  • Little appetite, or food that has no taste
  • Slow or incomplete elimination
  • A heavy body, with no wish to move
  • A lack of enthusiasm, as if life had lost its color

If you recognize yourself in several of these signs, do not be alarmed: your body is asking you to listen to it.

Where it hides

Your body is crossed by channels, the srotas, through which everything that lives in you flows, from food and blood to thoughts and emotions. Ama sticks to their walls like limescale inside a pipe: the water no longer reaches where it should, and what needed to leave stays behind.

Over time it settles into the seven tissues, the dhatus: plasma, blood, muscle, fat, bone, marrow and reproductive tissue. It stays wherever it finds a weak spot, and there, in silence, begins what years later we call illness. By then it will not leave just because we eat better for a few days: it has to be softened, loosened and carried out.

TWO PATHS

To soothe, or to cleanse from the root

Ayurveda knows two great ways of healing, and both have their place.

SOOTHING

Shamana

It brings the doshas back into balance without removing them from the body, through herbs, food, routine, gentle fasting, movement and breathing. It is the path of every day, and the path for those who do not yet have the strength for a deep cleanse.

PURIFYING

Shodhana

It removes what is in excess: it loosens ama and the accumulated doshas, carries them back to the digestive tract and eliminates them through the nearest exit. It asks for time, care and close guidance. Panchakarma is its heart.

Why go to the root

Soothing without cleansing is like cutting the leaves off a weed: the garden looks clean for a while, but the root is still alive. Tradition teaches that imbalances that are only soothed can wake up again, and that this is why, when there is strength for it, it is wise to cleanse from the root.

Panchakarma means “five actions” (pancha is five; karma, action), and it is a path in three movements: preparation, cleansing and return.

PURVAKARMA · THE PREPARATION

Before cleansing, we prepare

No one removes an old stain by scrubbing it dry. First you moisten it, soften it, let it soak.

That is Purvakarma (purva means “before”), a preparation as important as the cleansing itself. It has three stages.

Kindling the fire

First, agni must be awakened and the most superficial ama digested. For a few days your food becomes simple, warm and light, you drink hot water, and I give you herbs that kindle the fire (deepana) and digest ama (pachana), such as ginger, long pepper or cumin. The body is ready when the tongue clears, true hunger returns and a new lightness appears.

Snehana, oil and love

Then comes oleation, snehana. In Sanskrit, sneha means oil, and it also means love. This is no accident: oil does for the body what love does for the soul. It softens what is hard, loosens what is stuck and gives the body the safety it needs to let go.

Inside, for several days, you take ghee (clarified butter) in the morning, sometimes prepared with herbs, in the amount I indicate each day, according to how your body responds. Ghee reaches the deepest tissues and loosens ama the way oil loosens old grease in a pan. I watch for the signs that, according to tradition, mark the right point: softer skin, a digestion that receives it without effort, a slight aversion to ghee.

Outside, you receive abhyanga, a slow massage with warm oils chosen for your dosha: warmer and more nourishing for vata, cooler for pitta, lighter and more stimulating for kapha.

Swedana, the heat that opens

Once the tissues are oiled through, the heat comes. Swedana is herbal steam, with the head kept outside and cool, as tradition asks. The moist heat opens the srotas, and what the oil loosened becomes liquid. Then the doshas travel from the periphery (the skin, the muscles, the joints) toward the digestive tract, from where they can leave, like snow that melts and runs down to the river.

PRADHANAKARMA · THE FIVE ACTIONS

Five doors out

Once what was held has reached the digestive tract, the body can let it go through the nearest door. Pradhanakarma means “the main action,” and tradition describes five. I describe them to you as tradition teaches them, so that you know the whole map; your own path, as you will see, is traced to your measure.

KAPHA · THE UPPER TRACT

Vamana

Tradition describes it as the cleansing of the upper tract (the stomach, the chest, the airways), where kapha lives, the dosha of water and earth: on a carefully chosen morning, the accumulated kapha is released by the upper path. The texts associate it with spring, and with mucus, heaviness in the chest, lethargy or a sadness that will not move.

PITTA · LIVER AND SMALL INTESTINE

Virechana

Tradition describes it as a gentle purgation with herbs that cleanses the liver and the small intestine, the home of pitta, the dosha of fire, and indicates it when the fire overflows: burning, reddened skin, heat in the blood, a mind that flares up easily. Its season is autumn.

VATA · THE COLON

Basti

The texts describe medicated enemas given in series, alternating those of warm oil, which nourish, with those of herbal decoctions, which cleanse. They act in the colon, the home of vata, the dosha of air. Because vata is the wind that moves the other two, tradition calls basti half of all treatment, and indicates it for dryness, restlessness, light sleep and tired nerves.

THE HEAD · THE SENSES

Nasya

Oils and herbal preparations which, according to tradition, are received through the nose, the door to the head. It cleanses what accumulates from the neck up, above all kapha, and clears the path of prana, the vital breath. The texts indicate it for a heavy head, a blocked nose, dulled senses and a mind that longs for clarity.

PITTA · THE BLOOD

Raktamokshana

Its name means “releasing the blood.” The classical texts describe this action as letting out a small amount of blood when the blood has become loaded with heat and impurities, something that often shows in the skin. Today this purification is done mostly with herbs that cleanse the blood and the skin, such as turmeric, neem or manjishtha, and with food that cools.

YOUR PATH

To your measure

Panchakarma is not a list to be completed. According to your nature, your strength, the season and what I see in the consultation, I adapt the purification to you: how deep it goes and how long it lasts. The point is not to do a lot, but to do what is right for you.

PASCHATKARMA · THE RETURN

Coming back slowly

After the cleansing, the digestive fire is like a newly lit flame: alive, clean and still fragile.

Tradition teaches us to tend it like a new fire: first with dry leaves and twigs, then with thicker wood; a heavy log thrown on too soon would put it out. That is why Paschatkarma (paschat means “after”) begins with the plate, with samsarjana krama, the gradual return to food:

  1. Rice water. The warm liquid of rice cooked in plenty of water.
  2. Thin rice soup. A little more body; it begins to nourish without weighing you down.
  3. Kitchari. Rice and mung dal cooked together with gentle spices.
  4. Everyday food. Little by little, more varied foods, chosen for your dosha.

Taking care in those days

The body is left open and sensitive, like a freshly washed house with all its windows open: whatever comes in goes deep. That is why, for as long as I indicate, tradition asks you to rest and to avoid:

  • Cold, strong air conditioning and iced drinks
  • Wind and strong sun
  • Long trips and intense exercise
  • Late nights and daytime sleep
  • Talking a lot or loudly
  • Sexual intimacy
  • Holding back the body's natural urges
  • Strong emotions and arguments

When the fire is strong again, it is time to nourish. In a clean body everything goes deep, including nourishment. That is why Rasayana, the art of rejuvenation, comes after Panchakarma.

KITCHARI

The food of Panchakarma

If Panchakarma had a taste, it would be the taste of kitchari.

It is a simple stew of basmati rice and mung dal (hulled, split mung beans), cooked together with a little ghee and gentle spices: cumin, coriander seed, fennel, turmeric, ginger. In India it is the first food of many babies and the one offered to those who are recovering. It is a food of care.

Why it does so much good

  • Mung is the lightest of the legumes: it nourishes without weighing you down or causing gas
  • Rice and mung complete each other, feeding the tissues while the fire rests
  • It balances all three doshas: it suits almost everyone
  • Eating the same food for a few days lets agni rest, and that energy goes to cleansing
  • The spices kindle the fire without irritating it, and ghee carries their virtues into the tissues

A PROGRAM

How Panchakarma is lived

Tradition speaks of programs of seven, fourteen or twenty-one days, and sometimes longer. The length depends on your strength, on how much has accumulated and on the time you can give yourself; we decide it in the consultation.

The rhythm of a day

  1. On waking. You rise early, clean your tongue and drink warm water. On the days of oleation, you take the ghee I have indicated.
  2. Morning. The time for treatments: abhyanga, herbal steam and, when the day comes, the step of purification your process asks for.
  3. Midday. A warm bowl of kitchari when hunger comes, and then rest, without screens or hurry.
  4. Afternoon. A short walk, quiet reading, breathing, meditation.
  5. Evening. An early, light dinner, and early to bed.

What you may feel along the way

There are tired days, when the body is using all its energy to let go. Sometimes emotions rise for no apparent reason, or memories that have slept for years, and dreams become vivid. It is part of the process: what was held in the tissues, as it is released, sometimes passes through awareness before it leaves. And afterwards, many people tell me, lightness comes, and a calm they had not felt in a long time.

LETTING GO

What cannot be seen

Panchakarma cleanses the body, but it does not stop at the body.

Ayurveda never separated the body from the mind, or the mind from the spirit: what we hold, we hold everywhere at once. During a program life becomes simple, and when what is too much on the outside falls away, what is too much on the inside begins to show. Some people weep without knowing why, some release an old anger, some discover they no longer need to carry what they carried out of habit.

Letting go is not losing; it is making room. Tradition teaches that a clean body leaves more space for sattva, the quality of mind that is clarity, light and peace. And in that space, sometimes, we find ourselves again.

THE RIGHT TIME

When to purify

What accumulates, accumulates with the seasons, which is why tradition teaches us to cleanse as they turn; it is part of ritucharya, the art of living in harmony with each season.

  • In spring, when the kapha of winter begins to melt and the body feels heavy.
  • In autumn, when the heat gathered in the warm months asks to leave.
  • After long periods of stress, when body and mind feel weighed down.
  • Once a year, to keep the fire bright and the body light.

The right moment is personal. In the consultation I look at your tongue, your pulse, your strength and your life as it is today, and I see whether it is time to purify or whether we should first build strength.

GENTLE CLEANSING

Until the time is right

You do not need to wait for a full program to begin caring for your fire.

There are simple gestures that relieve the body of what it gathers day by day. They do not replace Panchakarma, but they prepare for it and sustain it. They are part of dinacharya, Ayurveda's daily routine:

  • A day of kitchari now and then, to let your digestion rest
  • Warm water, sipped through the day, instead of cold drinks
  • An early, light dinner, and an early bedtime
  • Fresh ginger tea to awaken the fire
  • Scraping your tongue on waking, from back to front
  • Eating only when you are truly hungry
  • A warm-oil massage before your bath, with the oil that suits your dosha
  • A few minutes of silence or meditation each day

In the consultation I help you choose the ones that are yours.

THE FRUITS

What Panchakarma can bring you

Each person lives their Panchakarma in their own way, but some fruits appear again and again.

Tradition teaches that whoever is well purified regains clarity of the senses, of the mind and of understanding, the color of their skin and their strength, and that old age comes to them slowly.

In the body

  • Lightness, as if the body weighed less
  • A digestive fire that comes alive again
  • More energy and stamina
  • Deeper sleep and rested mornings
  • Cleaner, more luminous skin
  • More ojas and, with it, stronger natural defenses
  • Aging more serenely, with more grace

In the mind

  • A clearer mind, without fog
  • Less inner noise
  • Better focus and memory
  • Senses more awake
  • A calm that holds

In the emotions

  • Emotions that weigh less
  • Less irritation and less restlessness
  • More patience with yourself and with others
  • The feeling of having let go of old burdens
  • More tenderness toward your own body

In the spirit

  • More inner space and more silence
  • Deeper meditation
  • More presence in what you do
  • Gratitude for life
  • The feeling of coming home

QUESTIONS

What people often ask me

How long does Panchakarma last?

Tradition speaks of seven, fourteen or twenty-one days, and sometimes longer; we decide the length in the consultation.

Is it uncomfortable?

Almost all of it is deeply pleasant: the warm oil, the massage, the steam, the rest. There may be a tired day or emotions that rise, and I accompany you at every step.

Can I work during the program?

Ideally, give yourself those days, because intense work and late nights take from the body the energy it needs to cleanse. Together we find a way that is possible in your life.

How often should I do it?

For many people, once a year is a good rhythm; some do it in spring and again in autumn. It depends on your nature, your age and your life.

What do I eat?

Mostly kitchari, warm and freshly made, with hot water and herbal teas: this way your fire rests and the body puts its energy into cleansing.

What happens afterwards?

The days of return come, with the gradual diet. Then comes Rasayana, to nourish and renew, and you take home a routine designed for your dosha, so that the lightness lasts.

Is it for everyone?

Not for everyone, and not at just any moment: deep cleansing asks for a certain strength. During pregnancy, in childhood, in weakness or in very old age, tradition prefers gentler paths. In the consultation I see whether this is your moment.
